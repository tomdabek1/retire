Where each year's money comes from

Before tax, if returns match your expected rate every year. CPP, OAS, pensions and rent are income. RRSP, TFSA and non-registered amounts are withdrawals from your savings. The part of each bar above the line goes to income tax, and anything left over is saved again.

What retiring earlier or later does

Chance the money lasts if you both shift your retirement by the same number of years, with everything else unchanged.

Which account to draw first

Each withdrawal order tested on the same 1,000 market paths. Automatic picks the best chance of the money lasting, and settles near-ties by what is left after tax: the median amount at the end, after tax on any RRSP still there. The order changes how much tax you pay, not what your investments earn.

What this simulator assumes
  • Everyone lives in Ontario. A joint scenario taxes each spouse on their own income; an individual scenario models one person on their own, with that person's accounts, their side of each property and its own spending figure. Tax uses the 2026 federal and Ontario brackets, the basic, age and pension income credits, the Ontario surtax and health premium, and the OAS recovery tax on income above $95,323. Brackets are assumed to keep pace with inflation.
  • Each simulated year draws a return at random around your expected return, using the swing you set. All accounts earn the same return.
  • In a joint scenario, pension income splitting is applied whenever it lowers your combined tax: up to half of workplace pension income at any age, and of RRSP or RRIF income from 65, moves to the lower-income spouse. RRSP withdrawals are drawn from whichever of you has the lower income first.
  • In a joint scenario, savings are drawn from the year you have both retired. Before that, the model assumes the spouse still working covers your spending, and it ignores any CPP, OAS or pension the retired spouse receives.
  • CPP is reduced 0.6% for each month it starts before 65 and raised 0.7% for each month after. OAS is $762.50 a month at 65 (October to December 2026), rises 0.6% for each month deferred, and rises 10% at 75.
  • Automatic tests the three withdrawal orders on the same 1,000 market paths and uses the one with the best chance your money lasts. Orders within half a percentage point of the best are treated as tied, and the tie goes to the one that leaves the most after tax, counting any RRSP still remaining as taxed all at once in the final year. It only chooses between these three orders, so it is not a full tax plan.
  • RRIF minimum withdrawals start at 71 using the CRA percentages. Cash you are forced to withdraw but don't need goes to your TFSAs (up to $7,000 a year each), then to non-registered savings.
  • Non-registered growth is taxed as a capital gain when you sell, at 50% inclusion. Each person's cost base starts at today's balance, and withdrawals come from each of you in proportion to your non-registered balances.
  • Rental income is entered for each of you, after expenses and before income tax. It keeps pace with inflation and is ignored in the years before savings are drawn. Each property's rent stops in the year it is sold. Sale cash is entered after commissions and tax and lands in the accounts you choose at the start of that year. RRSP and TFSA room is not checked. Sale cash put into an RRSP is deducted from that person's income once savings are being drawn, down to about the basic personal amount each year, with the unused part carried forward. Property values and any mortgage are not modelled.
  • Not included: what happens in a joint scenario after one of you dies (survivor benefits, and tax on one income), CPP sharing, spousal RRSPs, the Guaranteed Income Supplement, the Ontario low-income tax reduction, contribution room limits, investment fees, and tax on what you leave behind.

Figures come from the Government of Canada CPP page and TaxTips.ca tables for Ontario tax rates and 2026 tax credits. This is a planning estimate and is no substitute for advice from a financial planner.
